What a planetary alignment really is

All the planets orbit the Sun in nearly the same plane, so from Earth they always appear along one line across the sky — the ecliptic, the same track the Sun and Moon follow. That is why a group of planets looks like a “line-up”. What makes a parade is simply that several planets happen to be on the same side of the Sun as seen from Earth, so they are all above the horizon in the same evening or morning sky. NASA notes that “planet parade” is not a technical term, and Wikipedia that it is “not a true alignment in space”: seen from above, the planets are spread around the Sun, as the “from above” links on this page show.

Sizes are loose. Star Walk calls three planets a mini parade, four small, five or six large, and all seven a great parade, and counts every planet gathering in a single quarter of the sky only seven times in this millennium. Three- and four-planet gatherings happen most years; five or six every few years; all seven a few times a decade. Uranus and Neptune are always too faint for the naked eye, so a “seven-planet” parade is five you can see plus two you can find with binoculars or a telescope — which is why sources disagree about how many parades there are: Wikipedia counts the next six-planet parade after 2025 as 2040, while Star Walk’s list includes the telescope-only planets and finds six on 25 December 2027.

Planets are easy to tell from stars: they shine with a steady light and do not twinkle, and Venus and Jupiter outshine every star. Mercury is always low in the twilight and needs a clear horizon; Uranus (binoculars) and Neptune (telescope) need a chart or an app.

True alignments, seen from above

The orrery can also find alignments in the physical sense — planets bunched together around the Sun in heliocentric longitude, which is what a top-down view shows. These are rarer and older than any parade. Each link opens the orrery on that day, looking down on the solar system:

  • 7 March 1953 BC — the historic five-planet cluster recorded in Chinese chronicles, within a few degrees of each other around the Sun (7 March in the modern calendar; “February” in the Julian dates most books quote).
  • 3 February 2230 BC — the tightest five-planet grouping in five thousand years: within 7° around the Sun.
  • 21 August 1596 BC — all eight planets within 40°.
  • 3 October 600 AD — Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars within 1°.
  • 13 September 1306 — the four giant planets within 7°.
  • 8 September 2040 — the five naked-eye planets within about 9° of each other as seen from Earth, the tightest of the century.
  • 25 September 2114 — the inner four planets within 2°.
  • 7 April 2418 — five planets within 11°.

These come from scanning the same orbital model day by day across 3000 BC – 3000 AD; the whole list is in the orrery’s “Jump to a notable alignment” menu.

How to watch one

  • Evening parades: start looking about 30–45 minutes after sunset, towards where the Sun went down, then sweep left and upwards along the ecliptic. Morning parades: the same, 45–60 minutes before sunrise, towards where the Sun will rise.
  • Find a low, clear horizon in that direction — the innermost planet of the group (usually Mercury) sits closest to the Sun and sets or rises first.
  • Bring binoculars for Uranus and Neptune, and a planetarium app or chart to know which faint “star” they are.
  • The date is a peak, not a deadline: the arrangement changes slowly, so most parades are worth a look for a week or two either side.

Questions

What is a planetary alignment or planet parade?

Several planets visible in the same part of the sky at the same time, as seen from Earth. They appear along one line because they share an orbital plane; they are not lined up in space. Seen from above the solar system, the planets are spread around the Sun.

When is the next planetary alignment?

The card at the top of this page always names the next one. After the six-planet parade of 12 August 2026 come a small one on 14 November 2026 (Mercury, Venus, Mars and Jupiter before sunrise), five planets on 2 July 2027 (morning), six on 25 December 2027 (evening), five on 8 January 2028 (evening) and all five naked-eye planets before sunrise in late October 2028. The next parade of all seven planets is on 3 February 2034, and on 8 September 2040 the five naked-eye planets crowd into about nine degrees of sky after sunset.

How often do planet parades happen?

Three or four planets most years; five or six every few years; all seven only a few times a decade — 24 June 2022, 28 February 2025 and next 3 February 2034. Star Walk counts all the planets in a single quarter of the sky just seven times this millennium.

Do planetary alignments affect the Earth?

No. The combined gravitational pull of all the other planets on Earth is tiny compared with the Moon’s, whatever their arrangement, so alignments have no measurable effect on tides, earthquakes, the weather or people. Their significance is that several planets are easy to see in one sweep of the sky.

Can I see a planet parade from Australia, the UK or the US?

Yes — from the whole world. What changes is the local time and how high the line of planets stands above the horizon. Evening parades are seen after local sunset and morning parades before local sunrise, on the date given and usually for a week or more either side. The ecliptic stands steeper from the tropics, and on spring evenings or autumn mornings, which lifts low planets such as Mercury clear of the horizon.

What is the difference between a planet parade and a conjunction?

A conjunction is two objects passing close together in the sky — the great conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn on 21 December 2020, for example, which recurs about every twenty years (next in late 2040). A planet parade is several planets spread along the ecliptic in the same evening or morning sky; they can be tens of degrees apart and still count.
