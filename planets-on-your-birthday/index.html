How to read it

In the sky says where each planet was compared with the Sun. In the evening sky it was east of the Sun, so it was up after sunset, towards where the Sun went down; in the morning sky it rose before the Sun. More than 150° from the Sun a planet is near opposition and up most of the night; within 15° it is lost in the Sun’s glare. Whether it could really be seen also depended on where you were, which the night sky links work out for any place.

Constellation is the one of the 88 official constellations the planet was in front of, as seen from Earth. Brightness is the magnitude: smaller numbers are brighter, and from a dark site the eye can see down to about 6. Retrograde means the planet was drifting westwards against the stars for a few weeks or months, because the Earth was overtaking it (or, for Mercury and Venus, it was overtaking the Earth); the planet itself never turns back.

Questions

Where were the planets on the day I was born?

Enter your date of birth at the top of this page. For that day it gives each planet’s place in the sky: in the evening or morning sky or too close to the Sun to see, the constellation it was in, how bright it was, how far it was from Earth and whether it was moving retrograde. It also gives the Sun’s and Moon’s constellations, the Moon’s phase, a map of the planets around the Sun, and your age on each planet, with links to the 3D orrery and the night sky page on that date. Any date from 1000 to 3000 AD works.

What was the Moon’s phase on the day I was born?

The results give the Moon’s phase, how much of it was lit and the constellation it was in, at noon UTC on your date. The lit share changes by up to about 11 percentage points a day, so for the hour you were born, open the night sky link for your date, choose the place and move the time slider.

Which planets could I see on the night I was born?

Planets in the evening sky were up after sunset, those in the morning sky before sunrise, and any up most of the night from dusk to dawn. Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are bright enough to see with the naked eye; Uranus is at the limit from a dark site, and Neptune needs binoculars or a telescope. For when each one rose and set where you were born, follow the night sky links in the results and choose that place.

Were the planets aligned when I was born?

The results say how spread out the five bright planets were, as seen from Earth, and how that compares with every day from 1900 to 2100. Half the time they are spread across more than 170° of sky, and they fit within 60° on only about 2% of days. The tightest groupings in those two centuries are 5 February 1962 (16°), 17 May 2000 (19°) and 8 September 2040 (under 10°), and close groupings often sit near the Sun, where they are hard to see. The planets never line up exactly: see the perfect alignment.

Why does it put the Sun in a different constellation from my star sign?

Because star signs and constellations are different things. Astrology’s signs are twelve equal slices of the Sun’s yearly path, counted from the March equinox, and they last matched the constellations of the same names about 2,000 years ago. Since then the slow wobble of the Earth’s axis has moved the equinox about 30° along the sky, and the real constellations are unequal in size. So the Sun is in the constellation its sign is named after on only about one day in eight. On most days it is in the one before, and from about 30 November to 17 December it is in Ophiuchus, which has no sign.

Is this a horoscope or a birth chart?

No. It shows where the planets really were, in space and in the sky, as an astronomer would describe them. It does not use star signs, houses or aspects, and it makes no predictions.

How old am I on Mars?

Your age on another planet is the time since you were born divided by that planet’s year: 88 days on Mercury, 225 on Venus, 687 on Mars (1.88 Earth years), 11.9 Earth years on Jupiter and 29.5 on Saturn. Someone who is 30 is about 16 in Mars years and 2.5 in Jupiter years. The results work it out for every planet and give the date of your next birthday on each.

Does the time of day I was born make a difference?

Not for the planets: none moves more than about 2° across the sky in a day, and most far less. The Moon moves about 13° a day, roughly its own width every hour, so its phase and constellation can change during the day. The page uses noon UTC.

How accurate is it?

The Sun, Moon and planets come from the same model as the night sky page: the VSOP87 planetary theory and Meeus’s lunar theory, which agree with NASA JPL’s Horizons ephemeris to within 0.002° between 1990 and 2060. Before 1800 and after 2200 they may be out by up to about half a degree. The map from above uses the 3D orrery’s own orbital elements, so it matches what the orrery shows. Dates are in the modern (Gregorian) calendar. It is an educational tool, not a professional ephemeris.
