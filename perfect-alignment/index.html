The perfect alignment? Will all eight planets ever line up

Every few years a headline promises that “the planets are aligning”. Seen from above the Sun, they only ever come close. There are two ways they could: all eight strung out on one side of the Sun, from the Sun out to Neptune, or all eight along one line through the Sun with some on each side. The planets orbit at unrelated speeds, so neither is ever expected to happen exactly; the best they manage is to bunch into a narrow wedge or bow tie, and the tighter you ask for, the longer you wait. This page puts numbers on both, from the orrery’s own 6,000 years, from 20,000 years of JPL data, and from a two-million-year computer integration built for this question.

Short answer. All on one side of the Sun, the tightest grouping of all eight planets in the 6,000 years from 3000 BC to 3000 AD is 40° wide, on 21 August 1596 BC. In 20,000 years of JPL data it is 39°, on 27 May 5753 AD. In a search of two million years it is 17°, around February 863337 AD. A model that keeps today’s average orbital speeds puts one within 10° about once every 54 million years and within 5° about once in 3.4 billion, most of the age of the Solar System.

Let the Sun sit anywhere on the line and a tight line is about 128 times as common. The best in two million years is 8.8°, in 141241 BC. In the same model, within 10° comes about every 420,000 years and within 5° about every 27 million, or every 100 to 250 million years for a line that is straight in three dimensions too. The next good one is 11 July 2079, all eight within 22° of one line through the Sun. No exact line is known or expected.

What “aligned” means here

Picture the Solar System from above, the Sun in the middle, and draw the narrowest pie slice that contains all eight planets, Mercury to Neptune. The width of that slice is the first figure on this page. It is measured in heliocentric longitude, the planets’ angle round the Sun, which is what the orrery’s top-down view shows. The widest it can be is 315°, with the planets evenly spaced; 180° or less means they are all on one side. Planets at the same longitude seen from the Sun are in heliocentric conjunction, so a slice of 0° would put all eight in conjunction, on one ray from the Sun as seen from above, with the Sun at one end and Neptune at the other. This page calls that the perfect alignment.

The second figure lets the Sun sit anywhere on the line. Draw the narrowest bow tie through the Sun, two opposite slices of the same width, that contains all eight. Planets on opposite sides are in heliocentric opposition. At 0° this would be one line through the Sun, seen from above, with planets on both sides of it.

A syzygy (say SIZ-ih-jee) is the astronomers’ word for three or more bodies in a straight line. It does not mind which body is in the middle: the Moon is in syzygy at full moon, with the Earth between it and the Sun, as well as at new moon. A figure of 0° seen from above would not quite be one, because a straight line in space also needs the planets’ heights above and below the plane to agree: see straight in three dimensions below. Every one-sided grouping is also a line through the Sun, so the second count includes the first.

Both are different from a planet parade, where several planets appear close together in Earth’s evening or morning sky. Parades depend on where the Earth is and happen every few years. A line seen from the Sun does not care where the Earth is, and the tight ones are rare.

With the Sun in the middle: lines through the Sun

Let the planets sit on either side of the Sun and the best arrangements get much tighter. The narrowest bow tie in the whole two-million-year search is 8.8° wide, about half the best one-sided wedge.

Closer to home, the best line through the Sun in the orrery’s 6,000 years was on 16 March 878 AD, all eight within 15.7°: Mercury, Venus, Saturn and Neptune on one side, Earth, Mars, Jupiter and Uranus on the other. The best in JPL’s 20,000 years is 22 January 3322 BC, 13.4°, with Mars alone on the far side of the Sun; it is just outside the orrery’s fitted range, so it shows with an “approximate” note. The next good ones are not far off: 11 July 2079, all eight within 22° of one line with Mars and Neptune on the far side, and 17 February 2169, within 20.5°, with Venus, Earth and Jupiter on the far side. After those, the next within 12° is on 19 March 11603 AD, the tightest for more than 140,000 years either side.

How rare is each level?

The two-million-year run, together with a simplified model of the planets’ motion run long enough to count even the rarest groupings directly, gives a clean rule: the number of separate groupings tighter than an angle falls as the sixth power of the angle. Halve the angle you ask for and you wait 64 times longer. The reason it is the sixth: the share of time eight random directions spend within an angle θ falls as the seventh power, 8 × (θ/360)⁷, but the tighter a grouping, the faster Mercury sweeps through it, so each one is also shorter, and the number of separate occasions falls one power more slowly. In that idealised model, averaged over every possible starting arrangement of the planets, letting the Sun sit anywhere on the line multiplies the expected count by exactly 2⁷ = 128, one for each way the other seven planets can be shared between the two sides; actual counts come close to it (about 127 at 5° in the long runs).

The mean waits describe a simplified model, not a forecast. It keeps each planet moving at its present average speed, and it was searched exactly for every grouping over up to 3.6 trillion years of simulated time, so that even 5° groupings could be counted rather than fitted. Applying its rates to the real Solar System over millions of years assumes the orbits keep behaving like today’s on average, a reasonable guide that no calculation can confirm. A version with fixed elliptical orbits gives rates within about 1% at the wider angles; at 5° it has too few groupings to pin the difference down better than a few per cent. The two-million-year integration agrees with them for lines through the Sun at every angle it reaches. For one-sided groupings it happened to run a little busy, with 15 to 40% more groupings at 25° to 40° than the long-run rate: the luck of one window. An earlier version of this page fitted the rule to that window alone and gave 40 million years for 10° and 2 billion for 5°.

None of the long waits is a forecast. The Solar System is about 4.6 billion years old, so a one-sided grouping within 5° may have happened once or not at all, and the planets’ orbits are chaotic over tens of millions of years, so no computer can say when the next one will be. The tightest groupings anyone can date are the ones on this page, within a million years either way; anything rarer is a matter of odds, never of dates.

Straight in three dimensions

Everything above is measured from above, in heliocentric longitude, the way the orrery’s top-down view shows it. A line that is straight in space also needs the planets’ heights above and below the plane of the Solar System to agree, and the orbits are tilted a little to one another, Mercury’s by 7°. For wide groupings that hardly matters: within 20°, about 7% fewer lines qualify. For tight ones it bites. Within 10°, a line through the Sun that is straight in three dimensions comes about every 530,000 to 680,000 years rather than every 420,000, and within 5° about every 100 to 250 million years rather than every 27 million, because Mercury’s tilt is bigger than the whole allowance unless the line happens to fall near where its orbit crosses the others. One-sided groupings pay the same price, so a three-dimensional one within 5° comes less than once in the Sun’s whole remaining lifetime. The ranges show how much the answer depends on how the tilted orbits’ slow turning is modelled, with three approximate treatments tried; they are not a statistical confidence interval. Where the two-million-year integration has enough lines to compare, from 12° to 40°, it falls within them. The 141241 BC line is 8.9° wide in three dimensions.

Where the dates come from, and how far they can be trusted

Three different sources, each reaching further than the last and each less certain than the last.

  • 3000 BC to 3000 AD: the orrery’s own model. The orrery computes every planet from JPL’s long-range Keplerian elements, which are fitted to the planets’ real motion over those 6,000 years. Within that span its positions agree with JPL’s full ephemeris to a few hundredths of a degree for the inner planets and about half a degree for Saturn and Uranus. The orrery will still draw dates out to about 9000 BC and 13000 AD, with a warning, and the inner planets stay within about half a degree even there; the giants drift by a few degrees, and Pluto, whose elements carry no long-period terms, becomes meaningless.
  • 9999 BC to 9999 AD: JPL’s DE441 ephemeris. This is the same numerical ephemeris that spacecraft navigation uses, served by JPL’s Horizons system for those dates (DE441 itself spans 13201 BC to 17191 AD), and it is as close to ground truth as exists. Every event on this page inside that span was measured on it, from yearly positions with each orbit followed between them, and the 1596 BC, 5753 AD and 878 AD widths were rechecked on positions every ten minutes direct from JPL. The 5753 AD record, the 1596 BC and 1128 AD groupings, and the lines of 3322 BC, 878 AD, 2079 and 2169 come from here.
  • 998000 BC to 999999 AD: a purpose-built integration. To go further, the Sun, the eight planets, Pluto and the three largest asteroids were integrated forward and backward from JPL’s starting state with a high-order symplectic integrator, including general relativity, the Sun’s oblateness, the Moon’s pull on the Earth and the Sun’s slow loss of mass. Run across the 20,000 years that DE441 covers, it reproduces JPL’s longitudes to within about 0.0014° for every planet, which is the check that it can be trusted beyond. Its error then grows with time; the table below estimates how much.

The table is a sensitivity estimate, not a statistical error bar: it combines, under stated assumptions, the differences between variant runs of the integration (half the step size, no asteroids, no solar oblateness, the Sun’s mass-loss rate changed by about ten per cent, starting positions nudged by one part in ten billion). Most of the spread is one number: the rate at which the Sun loses mass to sunlight and the solar wind, known only to about ten per cent, which shifts every orbit a little faster or slower as the square of the time. Because that shift is shared by every planet, it acts mostly like moving the clock: a first-order check moves the 863337 AD grouping by about two days and leaves its width at 17.1°. The giants, which set the slow envelope of a grouping, stay good to a fraction of a degree for the whole million years, so the years in the tables above are reliable throughout. What blurs on the farthest dates is the exact arrangement of the inner planets: by 863337 AD Mercury’s own position is uncertain by up to about 18°, though much of that is the shared shift of timing rather than a change to the grouping. Read the farthest pictures as “what a 17° grouping looks like”, not as a forecast for that February.

Beyond a few million years the question stops having an answer at all. The inner Solar System is chaotic, with a doubling time for small differences of about five million years, so no starting data, however good, can say where Mercury will be in fifty million years. That is why the rarest rows in the table above are given as odds, never as dates.

Questions

Will all the planets ever line up perfectly?

Not exactly. No exact line of all eight, a perfect eight-planet syzygy, is known or expected: the planets orbit at unrelated rates, so the useful question is how close they come. All on one side of the Sun, the best is a 40° wedge in the 6,000 years from 3000 BC to 3000 AD (21 August 1596 BC), 39° in 20,000 years of JPL data (27 May 5753 AD) and 17° in a two-million-year integration (around February 863337 AD). With the Sun allowed in the middle of the line, the best in two million years is 8.8° (141241 BC). In a model that keeps today’s average orbital speeds, a one-sided grouping within 5° comes about once in 3.4 billion years and a line through the Sun within 5° about once in 27 million, or once in 100 to 250 million if it has to be straight in three dimensions too.

Does the Sun have to be at one end of the line?

No. Let the Sun sit anywhere on the line and a tight line is about 128 times as common at the same angle (exactly 128 in an idealised model, as an average over every possible starting arrangement of the planets), because each of the other seven planets may be on either side of it. A straight line of three or more bodies is a syzygy whichever one is in the middle: the Moon is in syzygy at full moon as well as at new moon. This page keeps the name perfect alignment for the one-sided case, every planet in heliocentric conjunction on one ray from the Sun, with the Sun at one end and Neptune at the other.

When was the last time the planets lined up?

All on one side of the Sun, the tightest grouping in recorded history was on 21 August 1596 BC, all eight within 40° as seen from the Sun; the last comparable one was in 1128 AD (40.2°), and the last within 20° about 550,000 years ago. Along a line through the Sun, the best since 3000 BC was on 16 March 878 AD (15.7°), and the best in JPL’s 20,000 years was on 22 January 3322 BC (13.4°), with Mars alone on the far side of the Sun.

When is the next planetary alignment of all eight planets?

On one side of the Sun, nothing close before 3000 AD: the tightest is 19 May 2161, within 69°. The next as tight as 1596 BC’s is 27 May 5753 AD, and the next within 30° about 40726 AD. Along a line through the Sun it is much sooner: on 11 July 2079 all eight are within 22° of one line, with Mars and Neptune on the far side, and on 17 February 2169 within 20.5°. The next within 12° is on 19 March 11603 AD, the tightest for more than 140,000 years either side. Planet parades in Earth’s sky are a different, far more common thing: see planetary alignments and planet parades.

How rare is a planetary alignment?

It depends on how tight you want it. In a model that keeps today’s average orbital speeds, all eight on one side of the Sun come within 40° about every 13,000 years, within 30° about every 74,000, within 20° about every 840,000, within 10° about every 54 million and within 5° about once in 3.4 billion. Along a line through the Sun it is about 128 times as often: within 20° about every 6,600 years, within 10° about every 420,000 and within 5° about every 27 million. Halve the angle and the wait grows 64 times.

Does a planetary alignment affect the Earth?

No. Venus at its closest raises a tide on Earth about one twenty-thousandth of the Moon’s, Jupiter about one hundred-and-seventy-thousandth, and the rest add less. Alignments have no significant effect on ocean tides, earthquakes or weather.

Why isn’t Pluto counted?

Pluto is a dwarf planet on an orbit tilted 17° to the others, and with a 248-year period it rarely joins the eight planets in a line. The figures here count Mercury to Neptune; the orrery shows Pluto too.

Sources and method

  • JPL Horizons — the DE441 planetary ephemeris, used for every date between 9999 BC and 9999 AD and as the check on the integration.
  • JPL approximate positions of the planets — the long-range Keplerian elements the orrery runs on, fitted for 3000 BC to 3000 AD.
  • Syzygy (astronomy) — the term, and that it covers conjunction and opposition alike.
  • The million-year run is a Wisdom–Holman symplectic integration (the SABA₄ scheme of Laskar and Robutel, 2001) of the Sun, eight planets, Pluto, Ceres, Pallas and Vesta, with the Sun-source relativistic terms, solar J₂, an averaged Earth–Moon quadrupole and solar mass loss, started from DE441’s state at J2000 and sampled yearly. Its sensitivity was estimated by re-running it with the step halved, without the asteroids, without solar J₂, with the mass-loss rate varied by about ±10% and with the starting positions nudged; the variant runs are kept with its data.
  • Groupings were found by scanning the positions daily for the narrowest wedge containing all eight planets (one side) and for the narrowest bow tie through the Sun (a line), refining each minimum to the minute (a numerical precision, not a claim of minute-level accuracy). Positions are geometric, where the planets are rather than where they appear from Earth, and “Earth” is the Earth–Moon barycentre. The same scans over the DE441 span and over the orrery’s own model give the dated events within 9999 BC to 9999 AD; the sources agree to a few hundredths of a degree where they overlap.
  • The mean waits come from a model with each planet moving at its constant average speed, searched exactly, with no time steps, for every grouping over spans up to 3.6 trillion years. For that model the expected rate of groupings within a narrow angle θ, averaged over every possible starting arrangement of the planets (a uniform-phase expectation), is θ⁶ × Σ|nᵢ − nⱼ| ÷ P⁷, summing the differences of the planets’ average angular speeds over every pair, with P = 360° for one side and 180° for a line, which is where the sixth power and the factor 2⁷ = 128 come from; the direct counts agree with it to about 1%. The three-dimensional figures come from the same search with the orbits tilted, with the tilts’ slow turning treated three ways. These rates describe the model; they are not a dynamical forecast over millions of years.
