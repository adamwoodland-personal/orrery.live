    About this sky map

    The sky above your horizon, from real data: the planets from the VSOP87 planetary theory and the Moon from Meeus’s lunar theory, both checked against JPL Horizons, and 1,637 naked-eye stars from the HYG catalogue. Drag to look around, pinch or scroll to zoom, and tap anything to find out what it is. On a phone, Point at the sky turns the map with the phone so you can hold it up and identify what you are looking at.

    Dates from 1000 to 3000 AD are covered; positions are most accurate between 1800 and 2200 and marked approximate outside that. Times are local to the place you choose. A planet counts as visible when it is at least 5° above a flat horizon while the sky is dark enough for its brightness; hills, trees and buildings will hide low ones for longer. Phone compasses are corrected for magnetic declination where you are, but can still be a few degrees out: move the phone in a figure of eight to calibrate, and drag sideways to nudge the view into line.

    Night sky tonight, from where you are

    This free sky map shows the sky above your own horizon tonight: the planets, the Moon, the brightest stars and the constellations, placed where they really are. It opens on your city and works out which planets are up after dark, when each is best placed, which direction to face and how high to look. Drag the time slider to watch the sky turn through the night from sunset to sunrise, or step to another night or another city.

    Point your phone at the sky

    On a phone, tap Point at the sky and hold the phone up. The map follows the phone, so the planet or star in the middle of the screen is the one in front of you, and an arrow shows which way to turn to find the planet you picked. Switch on the camera to see the labels over the real view. The compass is corrected for magnetic declination at your location.

    Which planets can you see with the naked eye?

    Five planets are easy to see without a telescope when they are above the horizon in a dark sky: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. Venus and Jupiter outshine every star. Mercury never strays far from the Sun, so it is only ever seen low in the twilight after sunset or before sunrise. Uranus is at the limit of naked-eye visibility from a dark site and Neptune needs binoculars or a telescope. A planet that is lined up with the Sun cannot be seen at all until it moves out of the Sun's glare.
