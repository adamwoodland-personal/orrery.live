About & credits
Real data: planet positions from JPL orbital elements; the background is 1,637 naked-eye stars (HYG catalogue) in their true sky positions. Orbit spacing & body sizes are scaled for visibility — the Size & Orbits sliders morph them to true scale relative to the Sun. Rotation is tied to the date at each body's true sidereal rate & direction (Venus, Uranus & Pluto rotate retrograde), so pausing holds a fixed orientation. The terrestrial planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are oriented from their IAU rotational elements, so the correct longitude faces the Sun — Earth maps to real geography. The giant planets & Pluto point their axes in the true directions — Saturn's rings and Uranus's sideways roll sit in their real planes — but their absolute facing is illustrative. The dwarf planets Ceres, Haumea, Makemake & Eris use mean orbital elements, so their orbit shape & tilt are real but their position along the orbit is approximate. Moons (toggle them in Show) use real orbit size, inclination, period & direction around their planet's true spin axis — Triton & Phoebe orbit retrograde, Saturn's moons share its ring plane, and the Moon rides the ecliptic as it really does. The Moon is placed by a lunar theory (longitude, latitude and distance, within ~0.1° of JPL Horizons over 1900–2100) on its real, slowly turning ellipse, and Earth itself sits at its centre rather than the Earth–Moon barycentre that JPL's element table describes. The other major moons follow their real mean motion on circular paths (their eccentricities are below 0.03), calibrated for the modern era (≈1900–2100); minor moons show their real eccentric orbits but their phase is illustrative. Planet orbit lines are redrawn as the orbital elements evolve, so each planet stays on its line even thousands of years from now. Zoom in on a planet to see its moons' orbit paths.
Dates & time: the view opens at the current moment, shown in UTC; dates are UTC in the modern (proleptic Gregorian) calendar, even before 1582 — history books usually quote Julian-calendar dates, which differ by days to a few weeks in ancient times. The slow drift between clock time and astronomical time (ΔT) is not modelled; near the ends of the range this can shift fast-moving Mercury by a few degrees.
Comets & spacecraft: comets use fixed orbital elements, so their orbit shapes are real but positions drift from truth away from the present (Halley's period is pinned to its 1986 and 2061 perihelia so that return lands on the right date). Spacecraft ride JPL Horizons trajectory data (launch era – 2049, extended on their final heading beyond); markers are symbols, not to scale. Because comet and probe paths span the whole system, the compact view maps their distances through the planets' own spacing — they cross each planet's displayed orbit exactly when they cross the real one. Trails trace each body's displayed path as time runs; the Upcoming events and the tooltips' "next opposition / best view" dates are computed live from the same orbital model (typically within a day or two of almanac values).

A free online orrery - an interactive 3D solar system model

Orrery.live is a free online orrery — an interactive digital solar system model that you explore in your web browser. Every planet is placed in its true position around the Sun using JPL orbital elements, so the view shows the real arrangement of Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto for any date you choose. Drag to orbit the camera, scroll or pinch to zoom, right-drag (or drag with two fingers) to pan, and hover or tap any body to read live facts about it. Double-click a planet or moon to lock the camera onto it and follow it as time plays.

It works as a solar system simulator and a live map in one: press play and the planets move on their real orbits, or pause and read off exactly where each one is today.

A time machine for the planets

Use the date picker or the time slider to scrub forwards and backwards through time, anywhere from 3000 BC to 3000 AD. Speed time up to watch the planets race around their orbits, or pause on an exact moment and copy a shareable link to it. The 'Jump to a notable alignment' menu travels straight to famous planetary alignments, planet parades and conjunctions so you can see exactly how the planets lined up.

Two companion pages put the same model into words: where the planets are right now lists every planet's distance, light time, evening or morning sky, retrograde motion and next opposition, and planetary alignments and planet parades gives the upcoming and past parade dates with links straight into the orrery. Where is Halley's Comet now tracks the comet live on its way back to its 2061 return, and what is an orrery? explains the name, the gears and the history behind machines like this one.

A live map of the solar system, from real data

The background is the genuine night sky: 1,637 naked-eye stars from the HYG database in their true positions, with optional constellation figures and named bright stars. Planet positions come from real orbital data, while body sizes and orbit spacing are scaled up for visibility — the same compromise used by physical orreries. The result is a live picture of the planetary sky: which planets are up tonight, roughly where to look for them, and when each next reaches opposition or its best viewing date.

Comets, spacecraft and sky events

Follow Halley's Comet, Comet Encke, Hale–Bopp and Rosetta's comet 67P on their real orbits, or trace Voyager 1 and 2, Pioneer 10 and 11 and New Horizons along trajectories from JPL Horizons. Tooltips show each planet's distance from Earth, light-travel time, where it sits in tonight's sky and its next opposition or best viewing date — and the events menu lists upcoming oppositions and elongations computed live from the orbital model.

Frequently asked questions

What is an orrery?

An orrery (pronounced OR-uh-ree) is a mechanical or digital model of the solar system that shows the planets in their correct relative positions and movements around the Sun. The name comes from Charles Boyle, 4th Earl of Orrery, for whom one of the first clockwork models was made around 1712. Orrery.live is a digital orrery - a free online, interactive 3D version you can explore in your browser.

Where are the planets right now?

Orrery.live opens on today's date, so the planets you see are where they actually are right now. Hover or tap any planet and its tooltip describes the planetary sky as seen from Earth: whether the planet is an evening-sky or morning-sky object at the moment, up most of the night, or lost in the Sun's glare - plus its distance and light-travel time from Earth and its next opposition or best viewing date.

How are the planet positions calculated?

Each planet's position is computed from JPL orbital elements, so the planets appear in their true heliocentric positions for any date between 3000 BC and 3000 AD. Orbit spacing and on-screen body sizes are scaled for visibility, but the angular positions are real.

How far back and forward in time can I travel?

Any date from 3000 BC to 3000 AD. Inside that range the positions are computed from the JPL data; outside it the simulation still runs but warns that positions are approximate because it is extrapolating beyond the orbital data.

Can I see planetary alignments, planet parades and conjunctions?

Yes. The 'Jump to a notable alignment' menu lets you instantly travel to famous planetary alignments - popularly called planet parades - and conjunctions, from tight five- and six-planet clusters to the moments the planets spread evenly around the Sun. It includes the planet parades of June 2022, January and February 2025, February and August 2026, and the next big ones on 25 December 2027, 3 February 2034 and 8 September 2040. You can also set any date yourself to check an alignment.

What are the stars in the background?

The background is 1,637 naked-eye stars from the HYG database, placed in their true positions on the sky — the real night sky, not a decorative starfield.

Why are the planet sizes and orbits not to scale?

A true-scale solar system model would leave the planets as invisible specks separated by huge empty gaps. Orrery.live keeps positions and orbital geometry real but scales body sizes and orbit spacing for visibility — and the Size and Orbits sliders let you morph the model to true scale to see the emptiness for yourself.

Is Orrery.live free, and does it work on mobile?

Orrery.live is completely free with no sign-up and runs in any modern browser that supports WebGL, including phones and tablets.
